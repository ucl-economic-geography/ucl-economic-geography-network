Seminar abstract: The world is facing a cost-of-living and ecological crisis. Housing is at the centre of both: it is a major source of affordability pressures and a significant contributor to carbon emissions and resource use. Most policy responses focus on increasing housing
supply through new construction. Much less attention is given to how existing housing is used and to the role that land rent extraction may play in creating inefficient and unequal outcomes. Existing debates typically propose two broad responses to land rent extraction: taxing land rents and decommodifying housing. However, these approaches differ in how they define rent extraction itself. This research develops a new framework for understanding and identifying extractive land rents. It addresses this inconsistency by starting from a positive definition of what land and housing should be used for: meeting housing needs within planetary boundaries, or housing sufficiency. Within this framework, rent extraction occurs when land and housing assets, and the value derived from them, are used for purposes other than meeting this goal. This framework is applied to examine the extent and forms of rent extraction in the contrasting housing systems of England and Germany using household survey data. The empirical approach combines descriptive quantitative analysis, statistical modelling and qualitative analysis of legal and policy documents. Novel housing space use thresholds are developed, enabling the mapping of housing space use in both countries. Land rent extraction is measured by estimating the market rental value of the housing stock, examining how this value is distributed, and whether it contributes to meeting housing needs. Finally, the framework evaluates the potential of two key policy approaches - property taxation and decommodification - within the institutional and political economy contexts of both countries.
